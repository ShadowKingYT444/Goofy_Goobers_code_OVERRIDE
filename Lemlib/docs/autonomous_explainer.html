How our robot drives itself

For the first 15 seconds of a match nobody touches the controller. The robot has to work out where it is, decide how to reach the next goal or stack, and choose how hard to drive each motor, about a hundred times a second. This page walks through each of those parts as they exist in our code today, with animations.

The big picture

Everything the robot does on its own comes down to three questions, answered over and over:

Question 1

Where am I?

Localization: an estimate of the robot's position (x, y in inches) and heading (the direction it faces, in degrees).

Question 2

How do I get there?

Motion control: turning a target such as "back the claw onto the stack" into a path: straight lines, turns and curves.

Question 3

How much power?

PID control: how hard to drive each motor right now so the robot arrives quickly without overshooting.

These run as one loop. Every 10 milliseconds the robot reads its sensors, updates its position estimate, works out where it should be heading, picks a motor power, and sends it to the motors. Then it does it all again with the new sensor readings.

1. Read sensorsWheel motor encoders, inertial sensor (IMU), GPS camera
2. Update positionAdd the latest wheel movement in the direction the IMU reports
3. Plan the moveDistance and angle to the target, or to a point on a curve
4. PIDTurn those errors into a power for the left and right wheels
5. Drive motorsThe robot moves a little, and the sensors see it
One pass takes 10 ms, so this loop runs 100 times a second.Passes: 0

We use two libraries: PROS, which talks to the V5 hardware, and LemLib, an open-source library that provides the position tracking, the driving motions and the PID controllers. Our own code adds the parts LemLib does not have: ending moves when the robot is pushed against something, slowing down for pickups, correcting position from goals and from the GPS, and the routes themselves.

About the animations: they are simplified models built from our code's numbers (gains, distances, thresholds). They show how each part behaves; they are not recordings of the real robot.

1Where am I? (localization)

The robot keeps one running estimate of its position and heading. Our match routes measure position from the starting spot: the start is (0, 0), the robot faces the wall with the toggle (heading 180°), and +y points away from that wall. Headings are measured clockwise, with 0° pointing along +y. The skills route uses field coordinates instead (inches from the field's center), and converts them to the same system.

Odometry: counting wheel turns

Our robot has no separate tracking wheels, so it measures distance with the encoders built into the drive motors. Each encoder counts how far its wheel has turned. Our wheels are 2.75 in across, so one full turn rolls the robot about 8.6 in (2.75 × π). The inertial sensor (IMU) measures heading by sensing rotation, much like the sensor that rotates a phone screen.

Every 10 ms the robot takes the small distance the wheels moved since last time and adds it to its position, in the direction the IMU says it is facing:

distance = average of (left wheel change, right wheel change)
x = x + distance × sin(heading)
y = y + distance × cos(heading)
Real robot and pathWhere odometry thinks it is

With perfect grip the estimate sits right on top of the real path. Turn on wheel slip: the wheels spin a little more than the robot actually moves, mostly in turns, and the estimate slowly drifts away. The heading stays correct because it comes from the IMU, not the wheels.

Odometry is smooth and updates instantly, but every small error adds up and is never removed. On our robot the biggest causes are:

  • Wheel slip. Omni wheels skid sideways a little in fast turns and when the robot pushes into something.
  • Turning with a stack raised. With the weight on the claw end, the robot pivots about a point behind its center. The center swings several inches and the drive wheels cannot see it.
  • Contact. Hitting a goal or wall can shove the robot without the wheels turning the same amount.

So we correct odometry from two other sources: physical contact with goals, and the GPS sensor.

Contact references: using the goal as a ruler

This is the reference we trust most. When the robot has just placed a stack on a goal, the claw's grip is sitting on the goal's center. The grip is 8 in behind the robot's center. The goal's position is known, and the IMU gives the heading, so the robot's center must be exactly 8 in from the goal along that heading, whatever odometry says. The code overwrites its position with that point.

Real robotOdometry estimate

Odometry thought the robot was a few inches off. Once the claw is on the goal, the position is reset from the goal: center = goal + 8 in along the heading. The heading itself is left alone, since the IMU is reliable.

GPS: an absolute position from the field walls

The VEX GPS sensor is a camera. The field walls carry a printed strip of black and white code, and the camera reads it to work out where it is on the field, the same way every time, without building up drift. It also reports how confident it is. On our robot the camera looks out of the robot's right side, with the lens 3.0 in forward of and 3.7 in to the right of the robot's center. The code shifts each reading by that offset so it describes the robot's center, not the camera's.

GPS readings jump around by an inch or so and can be wrong when the camera is blurred by fast spinning or when a wall is too close to see the strip. So we never just copy GPS into the position. A correction only happens at a checkpoint, and only if it passes these tests:

  • The robot is parked: wheels still, so the camera image is sharp
  • Readings the sensor itself rates worse than 3 in are dropped; the rest are averaged (about 6 readings in a quarter second)
  • The average is within 6 in of where the route expected the robot to be
  • The average is within 8 in of odometry (a bigger gap is more likely a bad reading)
  • Odometry's x and y are moved to the GPS average; the heading stays with the IMU

Separately, a background filter mixes every GPS reading with odometry for the live display on the Brain screen. It moves with the odometry between readings and is pulled about 20% of the way toward GPS every tenth of a second while parked (6% while driving). It ignores readings more than about 6 in from its current estimate, needs 3 readings that agree before it trusts GPS at all, and starts over if 8 "outliers" in a row agree with each other (which means the robot was really shoved). This keeps the display smooth while still being anchored to the field.

What we learned by testing: more GPS corrections did not mean more accuracy. Running the three-pin route back to back, the version with a correction at every stop missed stack 1 and the version without corrections did not. Continuous correction while driving made it worse too. The route now uses contact references after every score and a single GPS checkpoint, right after clamping stack 2, where the odometry has drifted the most. We also take resets at the same spot and heading every time, so any error in the GPS mount repeats and can be tuned out instead of changing from run to run.

2How do I get there? (motion control)

A route is a list of moves. LemLib gives us three kinds, and our code builds the rest on top of them:

  • Turn in place to a heading, or to face a point.
  • Move to a point: drive to an (x, y) while steering toward it.
  • Move to a pose: drive to an (x, y) and arrive facing a chosen heading, by following a curve.

Our claw is on the back of the robot, so every pickup and every score is driven backwards, claw first. The same motions work in reverse; the robot simply aims its back at the target.

Curves: the "carrot on a stick" method

To arrive at a point already facing the right way (for example square onto a wall stack), LemLib uses a method called boomerang. Instead of aiming straight at the target, the robot chases a "carrot" point placed behind the target, on the line of the final heading. The carrot sits a fraction (the lead) of the remaining distance back from the target. As the robot gets closer, the carrot slides in toward the target, so the robot swings round and lines up. Our curve tuner, which drives test curves on the real robot and measures the result, picked a lead of 0.45.

RobotCarrot point it is chasing7.5 in final zone

Straight: the robot turns to face the target and drives at it, arriving at whatever angle it came in at. Curve: it arrives facing right (east). A bigger lead makes a wider swing. Inside the dashed 7.5 in circle LemLib stops steering toward the point and only holds the final heading, which is why any sideways error left over from a curve is carried into the target.

Our two-pin routes use a curve onto the wall stack that was not guessed: we drove it by hand while a recorder (the UP button) logged the path, then turned the recording into waypoints and headings. A curve pushed by hand disagreed with a controller-driven one by 7 in, so we use the controller-driven recording.

Chaining moves: not stopping when you don't need to

By default a move ends only once the robot has settled: slowed down and stayed within 1 in of the target for a moment. That is accurate but slow: on the robot each stop cost about half a second to a second. When one move leads straight into another, we give the first one a minimum speed (40 out of 127): it never drops below that power, and it ends as soon as it gets within 2 in of its target (or crosses the finish line through it). The next move then picks up while the robot is still rolling. Turns that hand over end within 4° at a floor of 20.

Both robots run the same two 24 in legs with our PID gains. The top one settles at the middle point; the bottom one hands over at 40 power. The graph is speed over time.

Arriving gently at a stack

Arriving fast at a stack can knock it over before the claw closes. The robot's arrival speed is set by the PID gain (more on that below): close to the target, the drive power is roughly 6 × the distance left. So for the last 10 in of every pickup our code switches the gain down to 3 and sets a minimum power of 24, so the robot still creeps all the way onto the stack instead of stalling short. The claw closes 8 in before the robot's center reaches the stack, which puts the grip right on it.

Knowing when you have arrived

When the robot drives into a goal or a wall, it may be stopped by the goal a little before its target point. Waiting to "settle" would then never finish: the robot just pushes until the move's time limit runs out. That was the cause of a bug where the robot waited about a second at every goal. LemLib has no way to notice this, so we added our own check: when a move is driven into contact, the code watches the drive wheels, and if they spin slower than 12 rpm for 150 ms (after the first 300 ms of the move) it ends the move there.

We also send these moves to a point slightly past the contact point on purpose, so the robot always reaches the goal and the stall check decides when it is done.

3How much power? (PID control)

At every step the robot knows its error: how far it still is from the target (in inches for driving, degrees for turning). A PID controller turns that error into a motor power between −127 and 127. It adds up three parts:

P · Proportional

How far is left?

Power proportional to the distance left. Far away means full power; close means gentle. With our setting of 6, the robot is still at full power until about 21 in out (6 × 21 ≈ 127).

I · Integral

How long have I been short?

Adds up error over time to push through small leftover gaps. We set it to 0: it keeps growing while the robot is blocked and then overshoots. Minimum speeds and stall checks do its job instead.

D · Derivative

How fast am I closing?

Looks at how quickly the error is shrinking and pushes back against it, like easing off the gas before a stop sign. It damps overshoot.

power = kP × error + kI × (sum of errors) + kD × (change in error since last step)

The numbers kP, kI and kD are the gains. Ours are 6, 0, 3 for driving and 2, 0, 10 for turning. Try them in the simulation below, then try the others.

The model includes the motors' delay in speeding up and slowing down, and a little friction, so a very gentle controller stops short. It is a simplified model of our drive, not a measurement.

What we learned by testing: we tried an automatic PID tuner, which suggested 42 / 0 / 93 for driving. On the real robot that was far too aggressive: jerky starts and stops and overshoot. We went back to hand-set gains and removed the tuner. The gentle-zone trick above is the same idea applied on purpose: changing kP for one part of a move (called gain scheduling).

The same idea on the lift

The lift uses a simpler controller: power = 0.45 × (degrees left to go), read from a rotation sensor on the lift. Rubber bands pull the lift up, so going down needs more help: the power never drops below 110 going down or 60 going up, and the move ends within 8° of the target. Near the top (95% of full height) the code holds the lift at the top against gravity. Lift and arm moves run alongside driving, so the robot does not wait for them.

4Putting it together: the three-pin run

Here is our main match route with every technique labelled as it happens. The positions come from the route's code; the timing is approximate. Watch the orange outline: odometry drifts during turns with a stack raised and is pulled back by the contact references and the GPS checkpoint.

  1. Hit the toggle: back off the wall while the lift rises, then drive into the wall and stop on stall
  2. Score the preload: back out, turn, back into the goal, lower the lift onto the pin, open the claw
  3. Pick up field stack 1: aim the claw, fast approach, gentle last 10 in, clamp
  4. Score stack 1 one level up, then reset position from the goal
  5. Pick up field stack 2, then a GPS checkpoint while the lift rises
  6. Score stack 2 two levels up, then reset position from the goal
  7. Pull away from the goal. Done, inside 15 seconds
